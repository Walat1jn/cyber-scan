You can't hover over one like a link on your computer, but your phone camera will show you the web address before it opens anything. Read it first. We'll practice in a minute.

EXAMPLE

Red flags to look for:

  • Fake urgency. "Within 24 hours" is meant to make you act before you think.
  • Details that make it feel real. It names your device and browser. You just watched how easy those are to collect.
  • A strange link. Don't tap it. Open the company's real app or website yourself instead.

Pete's rule

Preview the link before you open it

When your camera sees a QR code, it shows the web address in a banner. That banner is your "hover." Here are a few codes. Look at the address and decide: open it or skip it?

Why this matters

Advertisers, data brokers, and attackers all use traits like these. Attackers use them to craft convincing scams, like a fake update message that names your exact phone and browser.

What you can do

  • Look to see if the sticker or QR code appears altered.
  • Check the URL when viewing with your camera.
  • Go to sites directly.
Ways to Protect Yourself
  • Preview every QR code. Let your camera show the address, then read it before you tap. If it looks off, skip it.
  • Read the name letter by letter. Typosquatting means a fake address that is one typo away from the real one, like a swapped or missing letter.
  • Check the part right before the first single slash. That is the real owner of the site. Anything in front of it can be a decoy.
  • Be wary of stickers on top of other codes. Scammers paste their own over parking meters, menus, and posters.
  • Never log in or pay from a QR code you didn't expect. Type the site's address yourself or use its official app.
  • Pick a browser with tracking protection. Safari, Firefox, and Brave all limit fingerprinting. Notice the gray "hidden" rows above.
  • Keep your software updated. Old versions make you easier to pick out and easier to attack.
